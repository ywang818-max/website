What Does Your Rent Budget Leave Out?

An interactive Pennsylvania county comparison with Plotly.

Adjust a rent ceiling, inspect counties, and see why lower median rent is only the beginning of an affordability comparison.
Author

Yixuan Wang

October 27, 2026

A student comparing places to live might begin with a monthly rent ceiling. That is useful, but a cheaper place can still be difficult for local renters to afford. Which Pennsylvania counties remain in a budget-based comparison, and what does that shortlist conceal about local housing pressure?

This post is for students and early-career renters learning how to compare places. It extends the county data used in Blog 5: instead of another set of maps, the reader can change the comparison itself. The goal is a more informed shortlist, not a recommendation of a particular apartment or neighborhood.

Start with the full comparison

I use the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2020–2024 American Community Survey five-year estimates, archived through Census Reporter. All 67 Pennsylvania counties have compatible estimates. Dollar amounts are expressed in 2024 dollars; these are pooled-period estimates, not October 2026 asking rents.

The horizontal axis shows median monthly gross rent, including utilities. The vertical axis shows the percentage of cash-rent households spending at least 30% of income on gross rent. Households whose ratios could not be computed are excluded from that percentage. Every dot is one county, with equal marker size so a geographically large or populous county does not automatically dominate the picture.

Try the explorer: lower the rent ceiling to $1,000, inspect Erie, then reset and inspect Centre. Turn on rent margins of error before judging counties close to your ceiling. The county selector and linked table provide keyboard-accessible alternatives to clicking dots.

A cheaper shortlist can still contain pressure points

With the ceiling at $1,800 and no size restriction, all 67 counties appear. Reducing it to $1,000 leaves 43. Those counties contain 297,858 households with computed rent burdens. Their pooled burden rate is 43.6%, below Pennsylvania’s 48.2%. This pooled rate sums burdened households and divides by all households with computed ratios; it is not an unweighted average of county percentages.

The pooled result does not describe every county. Erie remains inside the $1,000 screen, with median gross rent of $904, but 49.2% of its eligible cash-rent households are burdened. Selecting its dot reveals median annual renter-household income of $34,870, plus the numerator and denominator behind the rate. Lower rent alone therefore cannot establish a lighter local budget burden.

Reset the filters and inspect Centre. Its median gross rent is $1,207, and 57.0% of eligible renter households are burdened. It disappears below the $1,000 ceiling, but its profile explicitly reports that it is outside the filter. The display does not silently change its values or mistake an excluded county for missing data.

Use the controls to challenge the first impression

At a $1,000 ceiling, requiring at least 20,000 households with computed burdens leaves two counties. This changes the comparison toward larger rental markets; it does not establish that their estimates are statistically more reliable. The number of represented households is not the survey’s respondent sample size.

The uncertainty toggle supplies another check. Cameron’s median rent is $689 ± $116, while Erie’s is $904 ± $20; these are published 90% margins of error for median rent. Filtering uses point estimates, so counties close to a ceiling should not be classified confidently from a one-dollar difference. The explorer does not supply uncertainty intervals for the derived burden percentages or claim statistically significant county differences.

Interactivity adds value here because a static scatterplot cannot show every county’s income, denominator, uncertainty and budget-dependent inclusion clearly at once. Changing the ceiling and market-size screen updates the dots, pooled totals and exact-data table together. Hovering identifies observations; selecting a county keeps its profile visible for deliberate comparison.

The practical takeaway is to screen on price, inspect local income and burden, and check uncertainty before narrowing a search. County medians do not describe every rental, personal affordability or neighborhood safety. They also cannot explain whether wages, student populations, household composition or housing supply caused a county’s burden rate.
