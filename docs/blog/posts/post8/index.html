Can a 95% Interval Miss a 30-Minute Average?

A simulated waiting-time example connects bootstrap intervals, coverage, and p-values.

Simulation
Bootstrap
Statistical Inference
Resample one dataset, repeat the experiment, and test a claim to see what statistical uncertainty means.

Yixuan Wang

November 1, 2026

Suppose a delivery service claims its average wait is 30 minutes. A sample will almost never average exactly 30. How much disagreement should make us doubt the claim—and can a “95% confidence interval” still miss the true average?

This is a teaching experiment, not an evaluation of a real service. I simulate independent waits from a normal distribution with mean 30 minutes and standard deviation 5 minutes. Knowing the truth lets us check an inference method rather than merely trust its label. Readers familiar with averages can follow the three steps below.

1. Turn one sample into an interval

Start with 200 simulated observations. Their average is 29.66 minutes. To bootstrap, draw 200 observations with replacement from that same sample and calculate their mean. Some original observations appear several times; others are absent. Repeat this 5,000 times and plot the resulting means—not the individual waiting times.

The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95% percentile-bootstrap interval of [28.94, 30.36] minutes. The bootstrap approximates sampling uncertainty using the observed sample as a stand-in for the unknown population. It does not create 5,000 new independent datasets from the service.

Use Step 1 in the lab to inspect the distribution. Compare sample sizes, then change the confidence level from 95% to 90%. A lower confidence target usually buys a narrower interval, at the cost of more misses.

2. Ask what happens in new samples

One interval cannot tell us whether a method achieves its advertised coverage. In Step 2, I draw 2,000 fresh population samples, constructing a bootstrap interval separately within each. Each new sample receives 999 bootstrap resamples. The graph shows the first 60 intervals in generation order; the summary counts all 2,000.

At 200 observations and 95% confidence, 1,899 of 2,000 intervals contain 30: 94.95%. That is close to 95%, with Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.49 percentage points. Simulation noise explains why repeated runs need not give exactly 95%.

But the confidence label is not a guarantee. With only 40 observations, coverage falls to 92.65% in this experiment. Percentile bootstrap intervals can under-cover in finite samples. More repetitions would measure that behavior more precisely; they would not automatically repair the interval method. Skewed data, dependence, or biased sampling could create further problems not investigated here.

The interpretation is about the procedure: across repeated samples, approximately 95% of well-calibrated intervals cover the fixed truth. Once an interval has been calculated, it either includes 30 or does not. We have not assigned a 95% probability to that fixed parameter.

3. Compare the sample with a stated claim

In Step 3, test the two-sided null hypothesis that the population mean equals 30. The statistic measures the sample’s deviation from the claim in estimated-standard-error units:

t=x‾−μ0s/n.t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}.

I simulate 50,000 new samples under the null model, calculating this statistic each time. Orange regions mark the most extreme 5% of the null distribution when confidence is 95%. They are the rejection regions for a test with significance level 0.05.

For the displayed sample, observed t is -0.934 and the simulated two-sided p-value is 0.351. Blue bars identify null results at least as extreme in absolute value as observed t. This p-value exceeds 0.05, so we do not reject the 30-minute claim. That does not prove it true; a p-value is not the probability that the null is true.

Switch the claimed mean to 31 and inspect the changed result. This is a teaching comparison, not permission to search for a significant hypothesis after viewing data. The test and percentile-bootstrap interval are different procedures, so their decisions need not coincide exactly.

The lesson is to report uncertainty with the estimate, check a method through repeated sampling, and interpret evidence relative to a clearly stated claim. A confidence level describes long-run performance; a p-value describes how unusual the observed statistic would be under a model.
